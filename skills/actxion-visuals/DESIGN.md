# Still Frame: the ACTXION design language

**The photograph is the story.** Every image starts from one real, current frame of a person in the middle of the story: the face at the touchline, the stare after the red card. It runs large, in its own colours, never tinted, never decorated. The design's job is to get out of its way and add the one line that makes a fan stop and argue.

**One loud line.** One headline, set in caps in a modern grotesk (Instrument Sans Bold), tight and confident. The one or two words that carry the story sit on a marker block in the accent colour, set in dark type, like a sub-editor's highlighter. Everything else whispers: a monospaced dateline (Geist Mono) for the competition, the time and the label, and a serif italic (Instrument Serif) for the human part: a quote, a kicker, the question. Three voices, each with one job, and never more than about twenty words on the canvas.

**Colour comes from the frame.** There is no house colour fighting the photo. Each image takes its one accent from the photograph itself, from the shirt, the kit or the board behind the player. It appears in only a few places: the headline marker, the dateline dot, a thin rule, the quote mark. The vignette behind the type is a deep tone of that same colour, so a Manchester United card leans red and a Chelsea card leans blue. There are no solid panels: the photo always fills the frame.

**Space is the luxury.** Generous, even margins on a strict grid. Text sits on a clear baseline in the lower third or a single column, never centred for the sake of it, never crowded against the edge. Nothing overlaps, nothing is clipped, and every element earns its place. If a choice is between adding something and taking something away, take it away.

**Made, not generated.** These should look meticulously crafted, the product of a picture editor and a typographer who sweat every crop and every line break, never a template filled in by a machine. No gradients for their own sake, no glows, no glass, no stickers, no emoji, no outlines around everything. Each finished frame should look like it took hours of careful attention, and should still read in half a second on a phone.
